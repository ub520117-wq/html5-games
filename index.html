Game Guides — Rules, Controls & Winning Tips

New to a game? Each guide below explains the goal, the exact controls, and the strategy experienced players use. Read the guide, then jump into the player and try it yourself.

🐍Snake — the original arcade classic

ArcadeEasy2–5 min

Goal: Eat the red food squares to grow longer and score 10 points each. The game ends if you hit a wall or bite your own tail.

Controls: Arrow keys or WASD on desktop; the on-screen direction pad on mobile. The snake cannot turn 180° — plan your turns one step ahead.

Strategy: Early on, roam the centre where you have escape routes on every side. As you grow, travel in wide loops along the edges and never chase food into a corner your own body is closing off. The tail end is always moving, so a gap that looks blocked often opens a second later.

🔢2048 — the number-merging puzzle

PuzzleHard5–15 min

Goal: Slide all tiles in one direction; two equal tiles that collide merge into their double. Build your way up to a 2048 tile. The game ends when the board is full and no two neighbours match.

Controls: Arrow keys or WASD on desktop; the direction buttons under the board on mobile.

Strategy: Pick one corner — most players use the bottom-left — and keep your largest tile locked there. Use only three of the four directions so the big tile never floats away, and build a descending "snake" of tiles next to it so merges chain in order.

🧠Memory Match — train your recall

BrainMedium2–4 min

Goal: Sixteen cards hide eight matching fruit pairs. Flip two at a time and find all eight pairs in as few moves as possible.

Controls: Tap or click any face-down card to flip it.

Strategy: Work in a fixed pattern — sweep row by row instead of flipping randomly — and say each card's position out loud as you reveal it ("apple, top-left"). When you uncover a card whose mate you have already seen, take that pair immediately before flipping anything new.

🧱Breakout — paddle, ball and bricks

ArcadeMedium3–6 min

Goal: Bounce the ball off your paddle to smash all 40 bricks. You have three lives; every brick is worth 10 points.

Controls: Move the mouse or drag a finger to slide the paddle, or use the ← → arrow keys. Click the board (or press Space) to launch the ball.

Strategy: Where the ball lands on the paddle controls the rebound: hits near the edge send it off at a sharp angle, centre hits send it straight up. Use edge hits to tunnel up one side — once the ball is above the brick wall it will clear several rows on its own.

🐦Flappy Jump — one-tap timing

ArcadeHard1–3 min

Goal: Flap through the gaps in the green pipes. Each gap you clear scores one point; touching a pipe or the ground ends the run.

Controls: Click the board, tap the Start / Flap button, or press Space — every tap is one flap.

Strategy: Fly in a rhythm, not in reactions. Let the bird sink to the lower third of the next gap, then give one firm flap. Two quick panicked taps almost always push you into the top pipe, so after each flap, wait and watch before tapping again.

❌Tic Tac Toe — beat the computer

ClassicEasy1–2 min

Goal: You play X. Make three in a row — across, down, or diagonally — before the computer does. It wins if it can, and blocks your winning move if it spots one.

Controls: Tap or click any empty square.

Strategy: Open in a corner, not the centre. If the computer takes the centre in reply, play the opposite corner to set up a "fork" — two separate lines that each need one more X. A fork cannot be blocked in a single move, so it is the reliable way to beat a defensive opponent.

💣Minesweeper — pure deduction

PuzzleHard5–10 min

Goal: Reveal all 71 safe squares on a 9×9 board without uncovering any of the 10 hidden mines. Your first click is always safe. Numbers tell you exactly how many mines touch that square.

Controls: Tap a square to reveal it. Switch Flag Mode ON and tap a square to mark a suspected mine so you cannot click it by accident.

Strategy: Start from the numbers, not the empty space. A "1" touching only one covered square means that square is certainly a mine — flag it, and every other square touching that "1" is then safe to open. Work the edges of the revealed area and let the numbers eliminate each other.

How to reach the 2048 tile — the full method

2048Strategy

The corner strategy in our 2048 guide wins games, but only if you handle the three moments that break it. First, the forced fourth direction: sometimes the board leaves you no move except pushing your big tile out of its corner. When that happens, make the move that disturbs the corner row least, then spend the next several moves rebuilding the row before doing anything clever. Second, the blocked snake: if a large tile lands in the middle of your descending chain, do not merge around it — merge into it from the small end and let the chain re-form behind it. Third, the endgame: below roughly 2,000 points of empty space the game becomes about survival, not score. Stop chasing merges, keep at least two empty squares at all times, and prefer any move that frees a square over any move that scores. Players who reach 2048 usually lose a dozen games in the 1,024-tile stage first — that stage is where the corner discipline is actually learned.

Minesweeper patterns that actually work

MinesweeperStrategy

Expert Minesweeper is pattern recognition, not guessing. Learn these three and most boards open up. The 1–2–1: when a 1, a 2 and a 1 sit in a straight line along the edge of the opened area, the mines are under the squares diagonally in front of the two 1s, and the square in front of the 2 is safe. The 1–2–2–1: the mirror image — mines sit in front of the two inner squares' outer neighbours, and the two centre-front squares are safe. The satisfied number: whenever a number already touches exactly that many flags, every other covered square touching it is safe — this is the most common source of "free" squares mid-game. On this site's 9×9 board with 10 mines you will still occasionally face a true 50/50 guess at the very end. When you must guess, pick the square that touches the fewest unopened numbers — it carries the least chance of being a mine.

From Flash to HTML5: a short history of browser games

HistoryBrowser games

Browser games began in the late 1990s as tiny Java applets and exploded in the 2000s with Adobe Flash, which let a single file hold animation, sound and code that ran on almost any computer. Portals hosting thousands of Flash games became many people's first gaming library. Flash had real costs, though: it was a closed plugin, heavy on batteries, and never allowed on iPhones. When HTML5 added the canvas element, audio and fast JavaScript to the open web standards themselves, the same kinds of games could be built with no plugin at all. Adobe ended Flash in 2020, and the games that survived were the ones rebuilt in HTML5 — which is why every title on this page is written in plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript: no plugin, nothing to install, and the same code runs on a school Chromebook, a phone, or a desktop PC.

Snake: how high scores are really built

SnakeStrategy

Beginners chase the food; experienced players manage space. The real skill in Snake is keeping an escape route that is always at least as long as your body needs to turn around. A reliable method is the wall-follow: pick a wall and travel along it in a slow spiral toward the centre, so your body packs into neat rows instead of scattered loops. Cut across the middle only for food that is clearly reachable, and always enter open space from the side nearest your tail — the tail is retreating, so that side keeps opening up. The classic beginner mistake is the celebration dash: grabbing three foods quickly in open space, then realising the only way out is through a gap your own neck is about to close. If a food looks risky, skip it. Food respawns; your run does not.

Reaction games: getting better at Flappy and Breakout

ArcadeSkills

Flappy Jump and Breakout punish the same habit: reacting late and then over-correcting. In Flappy, fix your eyes on the next gap, not on the bird — your thumb learns the rhythm faster when your eyes are one obstacle ahead. Keep a steady internal count ("down… flap, down… flap") and only break it when a gap is unusually high or low. In Breakout, watch the ball only until it passes halfway down the screen; after that, watch the paddle and move it in one smooth motion to the meeting point instead of chasing the ball's shadow. Both games reward short, frequent sessions: ten focused minutes a day builds the timing far faster than one long frustrated session, because your last, tired attempts are the ones that teach your hands the wrong rhythm.

Why short puzzle sessions feel so satisfying

PuzzleBrain games

Memory Match, 2048 and Minesweeper share a design that makes them unusually good break-time games: the goal is obvious in five seconds, a full round finishes in minutes, and the difficulty comes from your own decisions rather than from timers or luck alone. Memory Match exercises recall of positions you saw only briefly; Minesweeper is pure deduction where every safe square is earned by logic; 2048 asks you to plan several moves ahead and live with the consequences of each slide. None of that makes these games medicine — they are entertainment first — but it does explain why a ten-minute puzzle break can feel more refreshing than ten minutes of scrolling: the game asks for your full attention, gives you a clear result, and then lets you stop.
